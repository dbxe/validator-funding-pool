# Security Model

This document is written for a reader with no history with this project, including a future auditor. Every claim below either names the file and function that enforces it or points at a documented section. Read it with the code open. If the operator and the participants use the supported commands as documented, the guarantees stated here hold; where a guarantee is weaker than it looks, this document says so rather than rounding up.

Nothing here is a promise about the contract's correctness. The contract is the audited artifact; this document describes the system built around it.

## 1. System model

Four components, with different trust properties.

**The contract.** `contracts/ValidatorFundingPool.sol`, and the optional sidecar `contracts/FeeRecipientForwarder.sol`. Frozen and audited. Its invariants are ground truth: a claim about custody or accounting that the contract does not enforce is not enforced by this system, no matter what a script checks. The contract exposes no upgrade path, no owner rescue, no arbitrary external call, no delegatecall, and no way to change `withdrawalCredentials`, which is `immutable` and set in the constructor to `_makeEth1WithdrawalCredentials(address(this))`.

**The operational scripts.** `scripts/*.ts` and `scripts/lib/common.ts`, invoked through the `npm run` entries in `package.json`. These are the supported path. They enforce cross-layer preconditions the contract cannot see — beacon state, chain identity, deposit-data validity, signer identity — and they fail closed. They are not a security boundary against their own caller: anything a script checks, a person can skip by calling the contract directly. What they buy is that the documented workflow cannot reach a bad state by accident.

**The endpoints.** An execution-layer JSON-RPC endpoint (`RPC_URL`) and a beacon REST endpoint (`BEACON_NODE_URL`). Both are read over the network and neither is authenticated. Free public tiers are sufficient; see §4.

**The operator's machine.** The host running the scripts. Trusted. See §2, "The script host".

## 2. Trust model

### The operator

The operator is trusted to generate fresh validator key material, supply valid deposit data, pay the 1 ETH predeposit, open funding attempts with the agreed participants and weights, submit the top-up, run the validator, and configure `fee_recipient` as agreed. Those are documented trust boundaries, not enforced properties; see "Trust Boundaries" in `README.md`.

**The operator can grief liveness.** They can decline to open a funding attempt, decline to call `topUpValidator()`, misoperate or abandon the validator, get slashed, and keep EL priority fees and MEV by pointing `fee_recipient` anywhere they like. `topUpValidator()` carries `onlyOperator`; no one else can complete an attempt.

The contract enforces only that the operator holds at least the 1 ETH predeposit as credited weight. Whether misoperation is self-punishing is therefore a configuration choice, and the generic recommendation is an operator target of at least 16 ETH — a majority of the 32 ETH validator. Losses to penalties, slashing, or missed rewards are shared pro rata by credited weight, so at half or more of the weight the operator bears at least half of any loss they cause. See "Trust And Incentives" in `README.md`.

**The operator cannot redirect participant principal.** Four contract mechanisms, together:

- Withdrawal credentials are `immutable` and point at the pool (constructor, `_makeEth1WithdrawalCredentials`). Every consensus withdrawal, partial or full, pays the pool. Ethereum consensus writes withdrawal credentials once, at validator creation, and a later deposit for an existing pubkey only increases balance — see the spec links in "Trust And Incentives" in `README.md`.
- Refunds do not require the operator. `closeExpiredFundingAttempt()` has no access modifier and can be called by anyone once `block.timestamp > fundingDeadline`; it moves each participant's `activeFundedWeiOf` into `refundableWeiOf`. `refund()` / `refundTo(address payable)` then pay `msg.sender`'s balance to a recipient `msg.sender` chose.
- Claims do not require the operator. `claim()` / `claimTo(address payable)` pay `claimable(msg.sender)`, computed from `creditedWeiOf` fixed at top-up.
- Exits do not require the operator after top-up. `requestExit(uint256)` admits any address for which `_canRequestExit` is true, which is the operator **or** any address with nonzero `creditedWeiOf`.

The operator also cannot dilute a participant's share after the fact: `creditedWeiOf` is written once inside `topUpValidator()` from `fundingTargetWeiOf`, and the function reverts `FundingTargetsDoNotMatchValidator` unless `totalCreditedWei` is exactly 32 ETH.

### Participants

Participants are trusted with nothing beyond their own funds. `_fund` reverts `NotParticipant` for an address not in the current attempt and `FundingCapExceeded` above that address's remaining allocation, on both the `fund()` and the plain-transfer path.

**A listed participant can grief liveness at zero cost.** `topUpValidator()` requires `totalActiveFundedWei` to be exactly 31 ETH, so one participant who is listed in an attempt and simply never funds blocks the top-up for everyone. Every other participant's ETH is then locked in the pool until `block.timestamp > fundingDeadline`, when anyone may call `closeExpiredFundingAttempt()` and refunds become available. The griefer spends nothing — not funding is free, and they hold no position to lose — while everyone else carries the opportunity cost of idle capital for the remainder of the window. The mitigation is the duration itself: funding windows should be intentionally short, which is why `README.md`'s "Trust And Incentives" says so and why `FUNDING_WINDOW_SECONDS` is a per-attempt choice. Nothing about it is enforceable in the contract; a participant cannot be compelled to fund.

The other participant-side surface is the post-top-up transfer window described in "Plain-Transfer Funding — The One Divergence" in `README.md`, which costs the sender more than it costs anyone else.

Participants are expected to verify before funding rather than to trust the operator's word; the list is in "Participant Verification" in `README.md`, and `scripts/fund.ts` performs the same checks against the live pool.

### Third parties

Anyone may deposit to the committed validator pubkey through the deposit contract, permissionlessly. This is a property of Ethereum, not of this pool.

- **Cost of the griefing.** One deposit, minimum 1 ETH — the deposit contract requires `msg.value >= 1 ether` (consensus-specs `solidity_deposit_contract/deposit_contract.sol`, matching `MIN_DEPOSIT_AMOUNT` in `presets/mainnet/phase0.yaml`). That is the whole price of raising the head balance above the predeposit.
- **Custody impact: none.** The deposit cannot change withdrawal credentials, so the ETH is withdrawable only to the pool. It is uncredited external capital: the pool distributes it pro rata to its own participants and never back to the depositor.
- **Operational impact.** It forces an interactive typed confirmation on every subsequent `fund` and `top-up`; see §3 and "Hard Failure On Excess Predeposit Balance" in `README.md`. A deposit of 31 ETH or more takes the validator to a 32 ETH effective balance, which makes it eligible for the activation queue (`is_eligible_for_activation_queue` in consensus-specs `specs/electra/beacon-chain.md`, `MIN_ACTIVATION_BALANCE` = 32 ETH in `presets/mainnet/electra.yaml`) and sets `activation_eligibility_epoch`. From that point the fresh-predeposit preflight fails permanently; see §5.

### Infrastructure: the EL RPC and the beacon endpoint

Neither endpoint is authenticated. This is stated in the code: `assertBeaconMatchesExecutionChain` in `scripts/lib/common.ts` closes with the comment that it catches endpoint misconfiguration and does not authenticate a dishonest beacon node.

Neither is confidential or integrity-protected either unless the URL says `https:`. Over plaintext `http:` to a non-loopback host, every capability described in this section belongs to anyone on the network path, not only to whoever operates the endpoint: they can rewrite validator state, rewrite pool state, and — on the plain-transfer funding path, which has no revert underneath it — turn one ordinary funding transfer into a donation. `warnOnPlaintextEndpoints` prints a loud warning at the start of every command when `RPC_URL` or `BEACON_NODE_URL` is plaintext to a non-loopback host. It is a warning and not a refusal because a node on the operator's own LAN over plain HTTP is a legitimate setup; loopback and `127.0.0.0/8` are not warned about at all.

**What a wrong or malicious beacon endpoint can cause.** It can lie about validator state and thereby defeat every beacon-derived check at once: it can claim the pool's credentials on a validator that has different ones, claim a fresh predeposit for a validator that is slashed or exiting, or report a plain 1 ETH balance for a validator that has more. Participants who fund on the strength of such a lie can lose principal to a validator that is not bound to the pool. There is no cryptographic defence against this in-repo; the defence is to point `BEACON_NODE_URL` at an endpoint you control or trust, and — since the deposit data and beacon state are public — to have more than one participant check independently.

**What it cannot cause.** It cannot move ETH. Custody follows the withdrawal credentials written on the beacon chain at validator creation, not what an API says about them. A lying endpoint can induce a bad decision; it cannot execute one.

**What the layered checks do buy.** They make an *inconsistent* or *misconfigured* endpoint fail closed rather than pass silently:

- Chain binding. `assertBeaconMatchesExecutionChain` requires the beacon node's `/eth/v1/config/deposit_contract` to report the deployment's `chainId` and the pool's own `depositContract` address. A beacon endpoint for the wrong network is fatal before any state is read.
- Two-state confirmation. Credentials are confirmed at `finalized` and re-confirmed at `head`, from separate requests; see §3. The settled state is a constant with no variable behind it, so the two reads cannot be collapsed into one head read by configuration.
- Node health. `assertBeaconNodeHealthy` refuses a syncing node, an optimistic node, and a node reporting `el_offline`.
- Shape validation. `assertBeaconValidatorResponseShape` and `assertBeaconSyncingResponseShape` validate every field the preflights decide on, at the parse boundary, and any violation is fatal and names the field. `slashed` must be exactly `true` or `false` — a missing or null value is never read as unslashed. Numeric fields must be canonical unsigned decimal strings within uint64 (`parseBeaconUint64`), which rejects the hex, sign-prefixed, and whitespace-padded forms `BigInt` would otherwise have accepted.

**What a wrong EL RPC can cause.** It can misreport pool state. `assertDeploymentIntegrity` re-derives all five pool immutables and both system-contract code hashes from the chain the transaction will actually execute on, pins mainnet's system contracts against constants derived independently of any RPC (`CANONICAL_SYSTEM_CONTRACTS` in `scripts/lib/common.ts`, with re-derivation instructions in the comment above it), derives the withdrawal credentials from the pool's own address, and compares the pool's runtime code against a local build — so an *honestly misconfigured* RPC, one serving a different chain or pointed at a different pool, is caught.

That claim does not extend to a dishonest endpoint, and the distinction matters as much here as it does for the beacon endpoint. Every one of those checks is a read, and every read goes to the same endpoint. An endpoint that reports the right `chainId`, serves the right pool address, and answers `eth_getCode` with the audited runtime satisfies all of them while relaying transactions against different state, or against no chain at all — the layered checks are consistency checks over one source, not an authentication of it. As with the beacon endpoint, the defence is not cryptographic: point `RPC_URL` at a node you run or trust, over TLS, and have more than one participant verify independently.

What it is not is a defence against a stale or dishonest view of the *right* pool, and the frequently made claim that the contract's own reverts bound every bad EL read is false on the default path. On the `fund()` calldata path it holds: a transaction composed against a stale read reverts, and the ETH comes back. On the plain-transfer path — the default whenever the connection signs with a Ledger — it does not. `receive()` accepts ETH in the `ToppedUp` state as pool proceeds rather than reverting, so an RPC that reports `Funding` for a pool that is already `ToppedUp` turns a single, ordinary funding transfer into a donation the sender recovers only pro rata. No double-send is needed, and no off-chain check catches it, because every check the script makes reads the same lying RPC. The plain-transfer path trusts the EL RPC's freshness; that is the price of the clear-signed device screen. Under an RPC you do not trust, `FUND_VIA_TRANSFER=0` restores revert-bounding.

**What a wrong EL RPC cannot cause.** It cannot change custody. Withdrawal credentials live on the beacon chain, and the ETH that reaches the pool is distributed by the contract's own accounting.

### The script host

**Trusted.** A compromised host defeats everything below the contract, and specifically defeats blind-signed calldata: the machine composes the transaction the device is asked to sign, and on a blind-signed transaction the device renders nothing the operator can compare against intent.

The sharpest instance is documented as a wart rather than a solved problem: `claimTo(address)` and `refundTo(address)` take the payout address as an ABI argument, so the device shows the pool as destination and `0` as value while the address that receives every wei sits in calldata the device does not render. See "The `claimTo` And `refundTo` Wart" in `README.md` for the mitigation ladder — prefer the no-argument `claim()` / `refund()`, which always pay `msg.sender`; `scripts/claim.ts` and `scripts/refund.ts` select them whenever `RECIPIENT` is unset or equals the signing account.

A hardware wallet still helps against a compromised host on the one path that clear-signs: a zero-calldata transfer renders destination and amount, and `fundViaPlainTransfer` in `scripts/lib/common.ts`, called from `scripts/fund.ts`, defaults to that path whenever the connection signs with a Ledger. Everything else in this repository is blind-signed today; the full table is "What The Device Actually Shows" in `README.md`.

## 4. Operational assumptions

- **A live mainnet execution endpoint and a live mainnet beacon REST endpoint are available when capital moves.** This assumption is why no bypass exists anywhere in the preflights: free public tiers of both are sufficient for the handful of read requests these scripts make, so "the beacon API was unavailable" is never a reason to waive a check on a capital path. The one place the assumption is deliberately not made is `request-exit`, where `assertBeaconValidatorReadyForExit` skips its preflight when `BEACON_NODE_URL` is unset — refusing there would let an unavailable endpoint disable the recovery path. The reasoning is in "Required Beacon Preflight For Exit" in `README.md`.
- **A terminal is available for the excess-balance path.** `confirmExcessBalance` requires `process.stdin.isTTY`. `fund` and `top-up` cannot complete over a pipe, from cron, or in CI once a third-party deposit has raised the head balance.
- **Ledger is the mainnet signing path, with `LEDGER_ADDRESS` set.** The `ledger` network configures no `accounts`, so no private key is read for it; `ledgerAccounts()` in `hardhat.config.ts` refuses to load the network when `LEDGER_ADDRESS` is unset, and `selectedNetwork()` recognises both the `--network ledger` and `--network=ledger` spellings so the guard cannot be bypassed by spelling.
- **The environment outranks the keystore.** Hardhat returns `process.env[name]` and skips the `configurationVariables` hook chain entirely when the variable is set, so a keystore entry is consulted only when the environment does not supply the value (`hardhat/dist/src/internal/core/configuration-variables.js`, `_getRawValue`). Keystore users must `unset PRIVATE_KEY RPC_URL` in the shell that runs mainnet commands; see "Encrypted Keystore" in `README.md`. The keystore is also skipped under CI.
- **`status` needs no signer.** It runs on the `read` network, which declares no `accounts`; the `rpc` network's `accounts: [configVariable("PRIVATE_KEY")]` is resolved on the connection's first JSON-RPC request, so a read-only command on `rpc` would fail for an operator who has no private key at all.
- **No environment variable waives any capital-entry preflight, and none changes one.** The three former override variables were deleted, not narrowed; `test/DepositDataRoot.ts` sets all three — along with two older bypass names — and asserts every capital path still fails closed. The claim is about the capital-entry paths, `commit-predeposit`, `fund`, and `top-up`. `request-exit` is the deliberate carve-out at both layers: with `BEACON_NODE_URL` unset, `assertBeaconValidatorReadyForExit` skips its preflight and `assertBeaconMatchesExecutionChain` skips the chain binding, because refusing there would let an unavailable endpoint disable the escape hatch. Nothing there puts new capital at risk; the cost of a wasted request is the EIP-7002 fee. No beacon-related variable changes preflight behaviour at all any more: `BEACON_CONFIRMATION_STATE_ID` was the last one, and it is gone. Its only legitimate non-default value was `justified`, worth about one epoch of freshness on a confirmation that happens once per validator lifecycle and is re-confirmed at head regardless — not worth a knob on the security surface. `BEACON_NODE_URL` selects the endpoint; nothing selects what is asked of it.
- **Two environment variables select the *subject* of the checks, and both are pinned or compared.** The sentence above is about what is asked; it is not a claim that the environment chooses nothing. `DEPLOYMENT_FILE` selects the deployment record, and the record names the pool that every check in §3 is made *about* — chain id, all five immutables, both system-contract code hashes, the canonicity pin, the forwarder binding, and the address capital is sent to. `DEPOSIT_DATA_FILE` selects the deposit-data file, which supplies the pubkey, signatures, and deposit-data roots that `commit-predeposit` commits, that `fund` requires the on-chain commitment to equal, and that `top-up` requires `committedPubkey()` to equal. Neither can waive a check; each decides what the checks are run against, which is a different and equally consequential thing. `EXPECTED_POOL` is the declare-and-verify pin for the first: set it and `assertExpectedPool` requires the record's `pool` to equal it before any other check runs, and `assertStillFundable` re-checks it immediately before a funding transaction is signed. The second is pinned by comparison rather than by declaration: the deposit-data file's contents are checked against the chain's own commitment on every path that uses them. Both resolved paths are printed before the file behind them is opened: `readDeployment` prints `Deployment record: <path>` in every command's opening lines, next to the active-signer line, and `readDepositDataFile` prints `Deposit data file: <path>` in each of the three commands that open that file. A run against the wrong record, or against the wrong deposit-data file, is therefore visible rather than silent.

## 5. Residual risks

Each entry: the risk, why it is accepted, and what would justify revisiting it.

**Stranded 1 ETH predeposit.** If a funding attempt never completes, the operator's 1 ETH is bound to a validator that will not activate, and the contract has no path to return it. Accepted: the predeposit is what makes the credential guarantee checkable before participants commit capital, and it puts the first loss on the party asking for trust ("Trust And Incentives" in `README.md`). Revisit if the protocol ever offers a way to withdraw a partially deposited validator's balance without activation.

**A third-party deposit of 31 ETH or more permanently closes the top-up.** It brings the validator to a 32 ETH effective balance, consensus sets `activation_eligibility_epoch`, and `assertFreshPredepositMutableState` then fails on every `fund` and `top-up` forever. There is deliberately no recourse *in the supported commands*: the preflight cannot distinguish a validator that was externally funded from one that was compromised, and a waiver broad enough to cover this case is the waiver that was deleted ("Environment-Variable Anomaly Override" in `README.md`). The pool's own 31 ETH is never sent; participants recover through the deadline and `refundTo()`, and the operator's 1 ETH is stranded as above.

Where the permanence lives is worth being exact about, because it is not where it first appears. It is a *script* preflight, `assertFreshPredepositMutableState` in `scripts/lib/common.ts`, and nothing else. The contract would permit the top-up: `topUpValidator()`'s preconditions — `onlyOperator`, state `Funding`, the deadline not passed, `totalActiveFundedWei` exactly 31 ETH, a balance covering the top-up plus outstanding refunds, `topUpSubmitted` false, and `totalCreditedWei` exactly 32 ETH — are every one of them execution-layer facts, and a third-party beacon deposit changes none of them. So this is §1's "anything a script checks, a person can skip", stated plainly: an operator who calls `topUpValidator()` against the contract directly, without the command, reaches `ToppedUp` and moves the pool's 31 ETH into a validator that already holds 32 ETH of somebody else's. The supported path refuses because refusing is right, not because the state machine forbids it; what the preflight buys is that the documented workflow cannot get there by accident.

The attacker's own terminal state is worse than the operator's. The validator can still be exited — `requestExit` admits the operator before top-up, precisely because the operator carries the predeposit exposure — and the consensus withdrawal of roughly 32 ETH lands at the pool's withdrawal credentials, which is the pool. Under the supported commands the pool never reaches `ToppedUp`, and both payout paths are gated on that state: `claimable` returns `0` outright for any state other than `ToppedUp`, and `claimTo` reverts `InvalidState` before it looks at an amount. So the attacker's 31 ETH arrives in a contract with no path to pay it out, to them or to anyone. Accepted because the attack costs the attacker 31 ETH permanently to strand the operator's 1 ETH permanently. Revisit if the cost ratio changes.

**The `ToppedUp` plain-transfer race.** A funding transfer that lands after `topUpValidator()` is accepted by `receive()` as pool proceeds instead of reverting. How much of it the sender gets back depends entirely on whether they were credited in the attempt that topped up: `claimable` returns `0` for any address whose `creditedWeiOf` is zero, and `receive()` in `ToppedUp` accepts ETH from anyone without checking. A credited sender recovers their pro-rata share and donates the rest. A sender who is *not* credited in that attempt — the case below where a stale transfer lands after the sender was dropped from a reopened attempt — recovers **nothing at all**. There are two ways to reach the window.

The first is a timing race: the sender has two funding transactions in flight, the first fills their allocation, the top-up lands, and the second arrives against a topped-up pool. That sender is credited in the attempt that topped up — filling their allocation is what let the top-up happen — so the loss is the non-pro-rata part of the second transfer. The mitigation is the double-send discipline in "Plain-Transfer Funding" in `README.md`, and `assertStillFundable` narrows but cannot close the window. The variant in which a single transfer sits unmined past the deadline, the attempt is closed, and a fresh attempt without that sender tops up is the total-loss case: the pool credits the sender nothing, so `claimable` is zero for them forever.

The second needs no double-send at all and depends only on the EL RPC. Every check `fund` makes about pool state — including `assertStillFundable`'s final re-read — reads the RPC in `RPC_URL`. An RPC that is stale, forked, or dishonest and reports `Funding` for a pool that is already `ToppedUp` lets a single, first-and-only funding transfer through, and `receive()` accepts it as proceeds. If that pool topped up an attempt the sender was credited in, they recover their pro-rata share; if it did not, they recover nothing. No pre-broadcast check can prevent it: the script cannot detect a lie with the same endpoint that is telling it, and the plain-transfer path has no revert to fall back on.

What *is* caught, after the fact, is that it happened. `assertFundingWasCredited` reads the mined receipt's own logs and requires a `ParticipantFunded` from the pool for the signer with exactly the amount sent; a receipt carrying `EthReceivedViaCall` instead is fatal and says the ETH was accepted as post-top-up proceeds rather than credited funding. That is detection, not prevention — the ETH has already moved — but it is evidence that does not come from a fresh read of the endpoint that may be lying, and it replaces a silent donation with a named failure at the moment it occurs.

Accepted because no off-chain check can *prevent* either mode, and because the transfer path is the one action a hardware wallet clear-signs. `FUND_VIA_TRANSFER=0` trades that device screen for a reverting `fund()`, which restores the contract's revert as the backstop under an RPC you do not trust. Revisit if the clear-signing situation changes enough that the transfer path stops being worth it.

**Forwarder balance stranded before top-up.** ETH that reaches `FeeRecipientForwarder` before the pool is topped up cannot be swept, and there is no rescue path or alternate recipient. `sweep()` reverts, with a different error on each side of the funding window: before `Funding` — `Uninitialized` or `Predeposited` — `receive()` reverts `InvalidState` outright, and during `Funding` the transfer routes into `_fund`, where the forwarder is not in the participant set and the revert is `NotParticipant` (or `FundingClosed`, if the deadline has passed and the attempt has not been closed yet). Only in `ToppedUp` does `receive()` accept the ETH, as pool proceeds. If the pool never reaches `ToppedUp`, that ETH is stranded permanently. Accepted because an empty, unconditional `receive()` is what makes the forwarder safe as a validator-client `fee_recipient`, and adding a rescue path would add an owner. Mitigated operationally: configure `fee_recipient` to the forwarder only after top-up ("Optional EL Rewards Forwarder" in `README.md`). Revisit never — the mitigation is exact, and the alternative is a privileged role.

**The beacon preflight cannot cover the last stretch to inclusion.** `fund` and `top-up` re-run the whole head-state preflight immediately before broadcasting and require the balance to be exactly what the full preflight settled on, which shrinks the exposure from minutes — the funding review, plus however long the operator takes to approve on the device — to the seconds between that recheck and the transaction being signed and sent. The remainder cannot be checked at all: from the moment the transaction is signed until it is included, a third party may deposit to the committed pubkey, and the validator may be slashed or become activation-eligible. Nothing off-chain closes that window, and the contract cannot see beacon state. Accepted because the consequences are bounded — custody follows the withdrawal credentials, which no later deposit changes (§2, "Third parties") — and because the alternative is checking nothing. Revisit if the protocol ever exposes beacon state to the execution layer in a form a deposit could be made conditional on.

**Pool authenticity rests on the local checkout.** Until the runtime-code check existed, every pool check in this repository was circular: the deployment record is written by whoever ran `deploy`, the live pool answers whatever its own code says, and comparing the two proves only that they agree. A pool that was never the audited contract — one that reports someone else's withdrawal credentials, or runs entirely different code — passed all of them. Two checks break the circle. `deriveWithdrawalCredentials` computes the credentials from the pool's own address instead of asking the pool, so a contract whose consensus withdrawals would pay a third party is fatal, and the derived value (never the reported one) is what deposit-data validation and both beacon credential comparisons use. `assertPoolRuntimeCodeMatchesLocalBuild` compares the deployed runtime code against the participant's own build of `contracts/ValidatorFundingPool.sol`, masking only the byte ranges the artifact declares as immutables so that constructor arguments may differ and nothing else may.

What that does not cover: it proves the deployed code is the code *this checkout* builds, not that this checkout is the audited source — verify the repository's provenance independently, and the pool on Sourcify. It says nothing about the operator's intentions, which are a trust boundary throughout. And the artifact is a local file: a host that can rewrite `artifacts/` can also rewrite the script that reads it, which is §2's "the script host" and not a new exposure. The candidate set is exactly one artifact — hardhat's build output for this checkout — and no environment variable can extend it: a candidate decides both the bytecode the chain code is compared against and, through `immutableReferences`, which bytes are ignored on both sides, so an externally supplied candidate can always be made to match and the check becomes decorative. `maskImmutableRanges` additionally requires the declared ranges to be disjoint and to total at most 2048 bytes, against the 608 a real build of this contract declares (solc emits a 32-byte range per immutable reference site, and the five immutables are read at 19 sites under both build profiles); a broader set would zero out the bytes the comparison exists to check. Hardhat 3 keeps only the last-built profile's artifacts, so a participant verifying against the profile they did not just build must rebuild with `npx hardhat compile --build-profile production` and re-run; the check reports which artifact and which solidity profile matched. Accepted as the strongest statement an in-repo check can make. Revisit if the repository gains a way to pin an expected code hash from a source outside the checkout.

**A stale or swapped deployment record moves every check onto a different pool.** The checks above are all *about* the pool the deployment record names, and `DEPLOYMENT_FILE` chooses that record from the environment. A record for an older pool, for a rehearsal pool, or for a pool somebody else supplied is internally consistent: the chain id matches, the five immutables match, the code hashes match, the runtime code matches the local build, and every command reports a clean pass — about the wrong pool. Nothing in the record itself is wrong; it simply describes something other than what the operator meant, and on the funding paths that means capital sent to an address they did not intend. It is the same class of exposure as `EXPECTED_SIGNER`'s: a value the environment supplies silently, which no consistency check can catch, because consistency is exactly what it has.

Mitigated at two levels. Every command prints `Deployment record: <path>` in its opening lines, next to the active-signer line and before any transaction, so the resolved path is visible on every run rather than inferred from the shell that launched it. And `EXPECTED_POOL` is the declaration that turns that line into an assertion: `assertExpectedPool` requires the record's `pool` to equal the declared address before any other check runs, and `assertStillFundable` re-evaluates it in the final on-chain re-read immediately before a funding transaction is signed — the same two-moment treatment the `EXPECTED_*` funding pins get, for the same reason, since on the Ledger path those moments are minutes apart. An unparseable declaration is fatal naming the variable rather than ignored. Accepted with those mitigations because the record is an operator input by construction and there is nothing inside the repository to check it against; a participant's independent verification is the second pair of eyes ("Participant Verification" in `README.md`). Revisit if pool addresses ever come from a source the operator does not control.

**The canonicity pin has never fired in anger, and cannot until mainnet.** `CANONICAL_SYSTEM_CONTRACTS` holds an entry for chain id 1 and for no other chain, so `assertDeploymentCanonicity` *warns* on every chain but mainnet and falls back to the weaker deployment-record consistency check. Every rehearsal — devnet, the local end-to-end harness — therefore exercises the warning branch and never the comparison, which means the mainnet deployment is the first live execution of the pin comparison itself. Two things reduce what that costs. The pin *values* are separately confirmed: the devnet's system-contract code hashes matched the recorded mainnet hashes byte-identically, and `test-e2e/local-chain.ts` installs the real deposit contract (from the consensus-specs creation bytecode) and the real EIP-7002 predeploy (from the go-ethereum genesis allocation) at their real mainnet addresses and refuses to start unless both hash to exactly the recorded pins — so a wrong constant is caught by the harness, on every run, without a mainnet connection. And the harness asserts the warning branch is *taken* and printed on chain id 31337 rather than arranging for it to disappear, so the fallback is covered too. What remains untested until deployment is the comparison's own failure path against live mainnet state. Accepted: pinning is only meaningful against the chain being pinned, and the values behind it are verified independently of any RPC endpoint. Revisit if a mainnet-forked run is ever added to the harness, which would execute the comparison for real.

**Blind-signed surfaces.** Every action except a plain-transfer `fund` is blind-signed on the device: `fund()` calldata, `claim()`, `claimTo(address)`, `refund()`, `refundTo(address)`, `requestExit(uint256)`, `commitAndPredeposit(...)`, `openFundingAttempt(address[],uint256[])`, `topUpValidator()`, `closeExpiredFundingAttempt()`, `sweep()`, and both contract deployments. The deployments are the weakest: a creation transaction has no destination address at all, so the device shows creation bytecode with constructor arguments appended and there is nothing to compare. Accepted with a compensating control: verify the deployment after the fact — read the five immutables back from the chain, compare the runtime code against a local build (which every script now does automatically; see the pool-authenticity entry below), and verify on Sourcify — before publishing the pool address. See "What The Device Actually Shows" in `README.md`. Revisit when `@nomicfoundation/hardhat-ledger` requests descriptor resolution.

**The hardhat-ledger derivation-path cache (upstream).** The plugin caches the address-to-path mapping in `<hardhat config dir>/ledger/accounts.json` and returns the cached path without re-deriving it on the connected device (`@nomicfoundation/hardhat-ledger/dist/src/internal/handler.js`, `#derivePath`). A swapped device or a device restored from a different seed will sign at that path with a different key and the plugin will not notice. Accepted because it is upstream behaviour this repository cannot change. Mitigated in two places: the operator is instructed to delete the cache file whenever the device or seed changes ("Ledger" in `README.md`), and `waitForSenderVerifiedReceipt` checks `receipt.from` on every transaction — which detects the mismatch after the transaction is mined, not before. Revisit if the plugin gains a re-derivation or verification option.

**Environment-over-keystore precedence.** A forgotten `export PRIVATE_KEY=...` silently outranks a stored keystore key, including an empty string, and the run signs with the plaintext key. Accepted because it is Hardhat's documented resolution order and this repository cannot override it. Mitigated by the `unset` instruction and the `keystore get` verification step in "Encrypted Keystore" in `README.md`, by `assertActiveSigner` printing the address that will sign before the first transaction, and by setting `EXPECTED_SIGNER` to the account you intend to sign with, which turns that printed line into an assertion on any network. Revisit if Hardhat adds a way to make a configuration variable keystore-only.

**The typed confirmation is not proof against the machine's owner.** Ordinary non-TTY execution is rejected and no environment variable, flag, or acknowledgement string substitutes for a typed answer, but a deliberate PTY wrapper such as `expect` or `script(1)` can drive any interactive program. Accepted: the check is aimed at accidents and ambient automation, not at a person determined to automate their own machine, and no local check can be aimed at the latter. Revisit never — this is a property of the technique, not a gap in the implementation.

What the same check costs on the other side is worth stating plainly, because it is cheap to trigger: a single third-party deposit of 1 ETH — the deposit contract's `MIN_DEPOSIT_AMOUNT` — onto the committed pubkey raises the head balance above the predeposit permanently, and from that moment every remaining capital operation, `top-up` included, requires a human at a terminal typing the observed balance back. Nothing is lost and nothing is at risk (the credentials are fixed at validator creation), but an operator who intended to drive the remaining steps from a script, a runbook automation, or a CI job cannot, and the confirmation must be answered afresh on each attempt because the balance is re-read and compared each time. That is deliberate — the whole point is that a person looks at the number — but it should not be discovered for the first time at the top-up. `test-e2e/Commands.e2e.ts` asserts the non-interactive refusal so the behaviour cannot drift.

**Account confusion on the `ledger` network.** `eth_accounts` returns the node's own accounts first and the device account last, and every script signs with the first (`@nomicfoundation/hardhat-ledger/dist/src/internal/hook-handlers/network.js`). `assertActiveSigner` now aborts when the active address is not `LEDGER_ADDRESS`, which closes the accidental case. The residual: the assertion runs in the same process as everything else, so it protects against a misconfigured node, not against a tampered host. The operational instruction to point `RPC_URL` at a node exposing no unlocked accounts still stands. Revisit if the plugin gains a way to make the device account the default.

**The command harness covers composition; two paths remain outside it.** This entry used to say that no test drove a command end to end. One does now: `npm run test:e2e` (`test-e2e/`, deliberately outside `test/` so the unit suite stays fast) runs each SUPPORTED COMMAND as a real child process — `hardhat run scripts/<name>.ts --network <network>` with a controlled environment — against a local `hardhat node` carrying the real mainnet deposit contract and the real EIP-7002 predeploy at their real addresses, and a deterministic mock beacon node answering in the shapes a real consensus client returns. Each case decides on the exit code and on the exact lines the operator reads, which is what makes it a test of the wiring rather than a second test of the helpers.

What it covers: `deploy` (record written, runtime-code check against the local artifact, the off-mainnet canonicity warning); `commit-predeposit` on both sides of the positive-absence proof; `open-funding-attempt` with a declared participant set and with its defaults; `fund` over the `fund()` calldata path and over the plain-transfer path, including the receipt-log credit confirmation on both; `close-expired-funding-attempt` before and after the deadline; `refund` and `claim` with a real balance and with none; `top-up`; `request-exit` against the real EIP-7002 predeploy runtime, both refused on a `MAX_FEE_WEI` below the live fee and accepted at the default cap; and `status` on the read-only network with no `PRIVATE_KEY` in the environment at all. The negative sweep drives an `EXPECTED_SIGNER` mismatch, an excess head balance against a non-TTY stdin, a slashed validator, credentials belonging to someone else, four separately malformed beacon fields, and an `AMOUNT_WEI` above the remaining cap — each asserting the refusal line, a non-zero exit, and that no transaction was sent. Every signing command asserts the print-always active-signer line; `status`, which signs nothing, asserts its absence. Two cases exist that a stub could not have reached: a funding transaction broadcast with automine off and mined after the deadline is crossed, which is the only arrangement that reaches `waitForSenderVerifiedReceipt`'s receipt-status FATAL at all — with automine on the node rejects a failing transaction at send and nothing is mined — and the `ToppedUp` plain-transfer race described above, reproduced by queueing the funding that completes an attempt and the top-up that follows it ahead of the command's own transfer in a single block.

What it does not cover, each with its trigger:

- **The Ledger hardware paths.** Every `:ledger` command, `assertActiveSigner`'s `LEDGER_ADDRESS` comparison against the device account, the account-confusion ordering of `eth_accounts`, the derivation-path cache, and the clear-signing screens are all outside a harness that has no device. The signer policy is unit-tested against structural fakes and the entries above document what the plugin does; nothing executes it against hardware. Trigger: a device-in-the-loop rehearsal before the first Ledger-signed mainnet transaction — a manual procedure, not a regression test, and it does not become one.
- **The true mid-flight top-up race.** The harness reproduces the *consequence* — an uncredited plain transfer, detected from the receipt's own logs — by controlling block composition directly. It does not reproduce the *cause*: a real mempool in which the sender's second transfer and the operator's top-up land in an order neither of them chose, nor the lying-EL-RPC variant in which `assertStillFundable` reads `Funding` from an endpoint that is stale, forked, or dishonest while the chain is already `ToppedUp`. Neither is falsifiable from inside a harness that owns the chain and decides what a block contains. Trigger: a testnet run with a second, adversarial RPC endpoint, or any change to `assertFundingWasCredited` or to the plain-transfer branch of `fund`.
- **The EL rewards forwarder sidecar.** `deploy-forwarder` and `sweep` are not driven end to end. `FeeRecipientForwarder` itself is unit-tested, including the reentrancy and stranded-balance cases, and `assertFeeRecipientForwarderMatchesDeployment` is exercised through `assertDeploymentIntegrity`; what is untested as a process is the two commands' own composition, including the deployment-record update that adds `feeRecipientForwarder` to an existing record. Trigger: before configuring `fee_recipient` to a forwarder on mainnet, or any change to either script.

The harness is an instrument, not an invariant: §3 gains no row for it. What it does is make the rows that are already there fail loudly when the composition around them breaks.

**The ERC-7730 descriptor is a placeholder and is not submitted.** `clear-signing/calldata-ValidatorFundingPool.json` binds to the zero address because no mainnet pool exists yet, and submission has preconditions that cannot be met before deployment: the real pool address must be substituted, the contract must be verified on Sourcify, and a test file with real calldata samples must be written. Its path expressions are structurally valid but unverified against a real wallet, and the whole-array form used for `openFundingAttempt` is the least-exercised part. Accepted because none of it affects the Hardhat Ledger path, which requests no descriptor resolution at all. See `clear-signing/README.md`. Revisit at deployment.

## 6. Out of scope

- **Re-auditing the contract.** `contracts/*.sol` is frozen and audited; this document treats its behaviour as ground truth rather than re-deriving it.
- **Consensus-layer correctness.** Deposit processing, activation, withdrawal, slashing, and EIP-7002 exit processing are Ethereum's, not this pool's. Where this document relies on a consensus rule it cites the spec.
- **Host compromise.** See §2, "The script host". A compromised machine defeats blind-signed calldata and this repository does not claim otherwise.
- **The validator key ceremony.** Generating key material, storing the mnemonic, and running the validator client are the operator's, outside anything in this repository.
